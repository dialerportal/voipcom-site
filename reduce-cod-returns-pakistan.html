How to reduce COD returns in Pakistan

Return-to-origin is the silent tax on Pakistani e-commerce. Here is why cash-on-delivery orders bounce back, what each return really costs you, and the tactics — led by automated confirmation calls — that pull your return rate down.

If you sell online in Pakistan, you already know the problem. Cash on delivery is how the country shops — roughly 75–95% of e-commerce orders are COD — and it is also where the money quietly leaks out. To reduce COD returns in Pakistan you first have to see the leak clearly: parcels that ship, travel, and come straight back unopened. That is return-to-origin, or RTO, and across the market it commonly runs about 20–35% of COD orders. Every one of those is your product travelling in a circle at your expense.

The good news is that RTO is fixable, and the biggest single fix is boring and cheap: talk to the buyer before you dispatch. An automated order-confirmation call — "press 1 to confirm, press 2 to cancel" — routinely pulls a return rate from the 30s down toward roughly 18–22%. This guide walks through why returns happen, the money at stake, and how to stack a handful of tactics so fewer parcels come back.

Key takeaway: You cannot cut RTO by shipping harder. You cut it by confirming intent before the parcel moves. Start with a confirmation call on every order, then layer address checks, WhatsApp, and advance payments on top.

Why COD returns happen

Returns feel random, but almost every failed COD delivery falls into one of four buckets. Name the bucket and you know which tactic fixes it.

  • Fake, prank or duplicate orders. No payment friction means anyone can place an order with a fake name or a competitor's address. Nobody is home, or nobody wants it. These are pure loss — the parcel just tours the country and returns.
  • Buyer's remorse. The customer ordered at 1am on impulse, saw the same item cheaper elsewhere, or simply cooled off by the time the rider arrived two days later. The longer the gap between order and delivery, the more remorse creeps in.
  • Wrong or incomplete address. A missing block number, an old address, a phone the rider cannot reach. The courier tries once or twice, fails, and sends it back.
  • No confirmation at all. The order was never verified with a human. The single strongest predictor of a return is a parcel that shipped without anyone confirming the buyer actually wants it.

Notice that three of these four are decided before the rider ever knocks. That is why confirmation, not delivery, is where the battle is won.

The RTO math: what returns really cost

It is easy to shrug at a 30% return rate until you put rupees on it. Let us walk a simple, illustrative example — plug in your own numbers as you read.

Say you ship 1,000 COD orders a month at an average order value of PKR 2,500. At a 30% RTO rate, that is 300 parcels a month coming back. Suppose each failed delivery costs you PKR 300 in two-way courier fees, plus packaging and the staff time to re-shelf it. That is roughly PKR 90,000 a month burned on parcels that earned you nothing — before you count the cash you had locked in stock that was on a truck instead of on a shelf.

Now bring RTO down from 30% to 20% with a confirmation workflow. You are down to 200 returns — 100 fewer failed deliveries a month, around PKR 30,000 saved, plus 100 units of stock freed to sell again. The calls that got you there cost a fraction of that: market rates run about PKR 3,000 per 500 calls (roughly $0.018–0.02 per answered call), and running them through your own cloud PBX folds it into one monthly bill.

Tactic 1: automated order-confirmation calls

This is the lever to pull first, because it works on three of the four return buckets at once. The moment an order lands, an automated call goes out to the buyer: a short recorded greeting in Urdu or English, then "press 1 to confirm your order, press 2 to cancel." Press 1 and the parcel ships. Press 2, or never answer after a couple of tries, and it never leaves the warehouse.

That one interaction quietly kills fake orders (no real buyer to press 1), catches remorse (people cancel on the call instead of at the door), and surfaces bad numbers (a dead line is a red flag before you spend on shipping). Because it is automated, it scales — 50 orders or 5,000, the calls go out the same way, and you get a clean list of confirmed orders to hand your courier each morning. More tactics that stack on top

Confirmation calls do most of the work, but the businesses with the lowest RTO layer a few more habits on top. None of these replace the call — they close the gaps it leaves.

  • Address & number verification. During the confirmation call, or by a quick follow-up, read back the address and confirm a working phone. A five-second check stops the "rider couldn't find it" returns cold.
  • WhatsApp confirmation. For buyers who never pick up, a WhatsApp message with a confirm/cancel reply is a cheap second net. Pair it with the call, do not rely on it alone — a lot of chats go unread.
  • Advance or partial payment on high-value orders. Ask for a small deposit or full prepayment on expensive items. It filters out non-serious buyers and shares the shipping risk. Keep full COD on low-ticket products where the extra step would cost more sales than it saves.
  • Blacklist repeat returners. Keep a simple list of numbers that have refused delivery before. When one orders again, require prepayment or skip it. A handful of chronic returners can quietly wreck your average.
  • Ship faster. Remorse grows with every day the parcel is in transit. Confirmed orders that go out same-day come back far less often than ones that sit for a week.

Putting it together

You do not need all five tactics on day one. The order that works: turn on confirmation calls for every order, verify the address on that same call, and start a blacklist. Then add WhatsApp as a backup and advance payments for your most expensive lines. Watch your RTO weekly — the number becomes the scoreboard, and most stores see it move within the first month.
